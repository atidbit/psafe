Most teams say they want to learn from mistakes. Fewer actually do it. When something goes wrong, people go quiet, the review turns into a hunt for who slipped, and the same problem shows up again a few months later.

One reason is easy to miss. Talking openly about a failure depends on how well people know and trust each other's work. If I don't understand what you do, I'm more likely to read your mistake as carelessness. If you don't think I respect your role, you're more likely to keep your slip to yourself. The relationships come first, and the learning follows.

## What the research shows

Abraham Carmeli and Jody Hoffer Gittell looked at this link in a paper published in the *Journal of Organizational Behavior* in 2009 (Carmeli & Gittell, 2009). They asked how organizations can help employees learn from failures, and they focused on the quality of working relationships.

They used the idea of *relational coordination*, which has three relational parts:

- **Shared goals:** people are working toward the same outcome, not just their own piece.
- **Shared knowledge:** people understand how their work connects to everyone else's.
- **Mutual respect:** people value what the other roles contribute.

Across two separate studies, the results supported their model. These three relational qualities fostered psychological safety, and psychological safety in turn helped explain the link between high-quality relationships and learning from failures.

In plain terms, when people share the goal, understand each other's jobs, and respect each other, they feel safer admitting what went wrong. That safety is part of what makes learning from failure possible.

A caveat is worth stating. These were field studies that show how these factors relate to one another. They do not test a specific kickoff question or prove that one practice causes another. Still, the core idea is practical: if you want honest talk after a failure, invest in the relationships before the failure happens.

## How to use this on your team

**1. Name the shared goal out loud.** At the start of a project, say what success looks like for the whole team, not just for each function. "We all win if the customer gets a working system by March" lands differently than a list of separate deliverables.

**2. Ask each person what they see from their seat.** Go around and ask, "What do you see from your seat that the rest of us might miss?" The scheduler sees different risks than the engineer. The front desk hears things the manager never does. This builds shared knowledge fast, and it signals that every role's view counts.

**3. Make respect visible.** When someone's input changes a plan, say so. "Maria's point about the handoff saved us a week" tells the whole team that each role matters, which is the mutual respect part.

**4. Use the same question after a setback.** When something goes wrong, come back to it: "What did each of us see from our seat?" People who already know their view is valued are more likely to share what really happened.

**5. Repeat it when the team changes.** New members don't share the history. A quick round of "what do you see from your seat" helps them join the relationships that make honest talk feel safe.

None of this is soft. It is the groundwork that lets a team look at a failure clearly instead of defending itself.

Whose view does your team hear least often?
