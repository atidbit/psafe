"Don't bring me problems, bring me solutions." It sounds like good management. It keeps the conversation moving. It asks people to think before they talk. It also teaches a rule that is hard to unlearn: if you do not have a fix yet, keep the problem to yourself.

Many people already believe that rule. In a 2011 paper in the *Academy of Management Journal*, James Detert and Amy Edmondson described implicit voice theories: "taken-for-granted beliefs about when and why speaking up at work is risky or inappropriate" (p. 461). Across four studies, they found that these beliefs are widely held and add to the explanation of workplace silence. The interview evidence included people holding back even suggestions meant to help the organization.

One of those beliefs showed up again and again: the sense that it is not safe to speak until you have solid data, a polished idea, or a complete solution. In 190 interviews at a large high-technology company, people described a fear of raising a concern without proof, a need to arrive extremely prepared, and a belief that they should not speak up unless they could offer a constructive solution (Detert & Edmondson, 2011, p. 466). To see whether this was just one company's culture, the researchers asked 185 people in executive education courses, with experience across many organizations, when speaking up feels risky or inappropriate. The most common belief in those answers was the need for solid data or solutions, named 48 times. People said it felt unsafe to speak when an idea was "not fully baked," or when "there is not a solution in hand" (p. 468).

A limitation is worth stating plainly. The interviews come from one firm. The open-ended survey comes from executive education participants, not a random sample of all workplaces. And the studies identify beliefs linked to silence; they do not test what happens after a leader changes how they respond.

Those beliefs can also outlast the events that created them. In the interviews, people often could not point to a specific incident that justified the fear, and some stayed silent even when a boss later said they should have spoken up (pp. 466–467). A slogan about openness will not undo the rule if the first response to a bare problem is still a demand for a fix.

The practical point still holds. People often see a problem before they know how to fix it. A broken handoff, a safety concern, or a process that keeps failing can be obvious long before anyone has a plan. If the price of raising it is a ready-made solution, those early signals arrive late, or not at all.

Leaders can reinforce the belief without meaning to. "So what's your solution?" is a fair question later in the conversation. As a first response, it tells the room that an incomplete concern is unwelcome. Making "bring me solutions, not problems" a team slogan does the same thing in official language.

A better first move is to hear the problem. When someone flags an issue, try "Tell me more" or "What are you seeing?" Let them finish. Then you can ask what they have already tried and what they need, and decide together what to do next, including gathering more data. Curiosity first does not lower the standard for a later decision. It changes the price of admission. You can still insist on evidence before you act. You do not have to require a solution as the ticket to mention the problem.

The next time someone brings you a concern without a fix, notice your first sentence. If it is a demand for a solution, try curiosity first. What would change on your team if a problem were welcome before the answer was ready?
