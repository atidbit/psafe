Most teams meet a new process for the first time on a day when it counts. The new system goes live on Monday. The new handoff starts with the next real case. The new meeting format debuts in front of the boss. Then leaders wonder why people quietly fall back on the old way.

Part of the answer is simple. Trying something new in front of others means looking slow, asking basic questions, and getting things wrong where people can see. That is an interpersonal risk. When the first attempt is also the real thing, the safest move for any one person is to stick with what they already know.

## What the research shows

Amy Edmondson, Richard Bohmer, and Gary Pisano studied this problem in a setting where routines are deeply set: cardiac surgery. In a qualitative field study published in *Administrative Science Quarterly* in 2001, they followed 16 hospitals adopting a new cardiac surgery technology that required real changes in how the operating-room team worked together (Edmondson et al., 2001).

Every hospital in the study had a top-tier cardiac surgery department with an excellent reputation and patient outcomes. Even so, they showed striking differences in how well they put the new technology into practice. The successful hospitals went through a different team learning process than the unsuccessful ones. The authors describe four steps: enrollment, preparation, trials, and reflection. Successful implementers used enrollment to motivate the team. They designed practice sessions and early trials to create psychological safety and encourage new behaviors. And they built shared meaning and improved the process through reflection.

In plain terms, the teams that changed well did not treat the first real case as the place to learn. They made room to practice first, and then they talked about what happened.

A caveat is worth stating. This was a qualitative study of surgical teams in 16 hospitals. It describes patterns in how successful teams learned. It does not test a practice-run program in a controlled way, and an office team is not an operating room. Still, the core idea travels well: people try new behaviors more readily when early attempts are designed to be safe.

## How to use this on your team

**1. Explain why first.** Before the practice run, tell people why the change matters and why their part in it matters. That is the enrollment step. People are more willing to look awkward for a reason they understand.

**2. Run a practice round where nothing is at stake.** Walk through the new process with a sample case, a test account, or a mock meeting. Say out loud that mistakes are expected and useful here. The goal is to find the rough spots before a customer, patient, or deadline does.

**3. Go first on the clumsy part.** If you are the leader, try the new step yourself in front of the group and narrate what confused you. It shows that fumbling during practice is normal, not a mark against anyone.

**4. Debrief right away.** Ask short, open questions: "What felt awkward?" "Where did you hesitate?" "What would make this easier next time?" Write the answers down and change the process where you can. That is the reflection step, and it turns practice into improvement.

**5. Keep the first real runs light.** Treat the first few live uses as trials, not tests. Check in after each one and keep adjusting.

None of this lowers the standard. The goal is still to do the new thing well. A practice run moves the learning to a place where mistakes are cheap and questions are welcome, so the real day goes better.

What change is your team about to make that it could practice first?
