Think about the last time something went sideways on your team. Chances are someone had a feeling about it beforehand. Maybe a number looked a little off, a customer sounded different on a call, or a handoff felt shaky. They noticed it, but they weren't sure, so they didn't say anything.

That kind of knowledge is easy to lose. People share freely when they're confident. When they only have a hunch, speaking up feels riskier. What if I'm wrong? What if I look like I don't know my job? So the half-formed idea stays in someone's head, sometimes until it's too late to be useful.

## What the research shows

Enno Siemsen, Aleda Roth, Sridhar Balasubramanian, and Gopesh Anand studied knowledge sharing among coworkers in manufacturing and service operations. Their paper was published in *Manufacturing & Service Operations Management* in 2009 (Siemsen et al., 2009).

They found that psychological safety is an important antecedent of knowledge sharing, but with a twist. How much it mattered depended on how confident employees were in what they knew. **The more confident people were, the less psychological safety mattered for whether they shared.** Put the other way, safety matters most for the knowledge people aren't sure about.

They also found that psychological safety went up as coworkers communicated more often. And people's confidence in their knowledge was related to how easily that knowledge could be written down. Clear, documented know-how comes with confidence. Tacit, hard-to-explain know-how does not.

In plain terms, your team will probably tell you what they're sure of no matter what. The hunches, the gut checks, and the "this might be nothing, but..." are what a safe climate brings out.

A caveat: this study shows how these factors relate to each other in the settings it looked at. It doesn't prove that one practice causes more sharing, and it doesn't test the specific question below. The practices here apply the idea. They are not results from the paper.

## How to use this on your team

**1. Ask for hunches by name.** In a planning meeting or a weekly check-in, ask, "What's a hunch you haven't said out loud yet?" Naming the hunch tells people that unfinished thinking is welcome and doesn't need to be proven first.

**2. Lower the bar for "sure enough."** Say it directly: "You don't need data to raise it. A feeling is enough to start." Then treat a hunch as a lead to look into, not a claim someone has to defend.

**3. Thank the hunch even when it's wrong.** If a concern turns out to be nothing, say, "Glad you raised it. That's exactly what I want." How you respond to the first wrong hunch decides whether you hear the next one.

**4. Talk more often, in smaller doses.** Since safety rose with how often coworkers communicated, short and regular touchpoints may do more than one long meeting a month. A five-minute huddle gives people more chances to mention something small before it grows.

**5. Pay special attention to tacit know-how.** The things that are hardest to write down, like a feel for a machine, a patient, or a client, are the things people are least confident sharing. Ask experienced staff, "What do you notice that's hard to put into words?"

A hunch is not a conclusion. It's an early signal. Teams that make room for it get more chances to catch problems while they're still small.
